Abstract

Complex problems demand insights from multiple disciplines, yet the structure of academic research pushes in the opposite direction. This creates a practical barrier: even researchers who recognize the value of interdisciplinary work often cannot afford the time required to map unfamiliar literatures, evaluate evidence standards, and identify where key terms have different meanings across fields.

The cost of this barrier falls hardest at the start of a project. Early framing decisions shape which variables get measured, which methods get used, and which literatures get cited. Once a research question is articulated in the language of one discipline, it rarely recovers its breadth. Retrofitting interdisciplinarity after data collection is difficult; the window for reframing closes quickly.

This chapter introduces the Cross-Field Inquiry Engine (CF-IE), a conceptual blueprint using generative AI that targets this early-stage bottleneck. Rather than automating literature review or screening tasks, CF-IE redirects AI's capacity toward surfacing adjacent disciplines, contested definitions, and alternative mechanisms before a project's trajectory becomes fixed. The goal is not polished synthesis, but outputs that encourage rigorous inspection and iteration.

The chapter examines how CF-IE fits within the current ecosystem of AI-enhanced research tools, details its workflow and design principles, and addresses the epistemic risks inherent in using Generative AI (GenAI) for research design. CF-IE does not claim to solve the structural incentives that reward disciplinary depth over interdisciplinary breadth, rather it seeks to make cross-field scanning early enough that it changes the trajectory of their projects, and practical enough that researchers actually do it.

1Cross-Scale Problems and Narrow Starts

The Disciplinary Paradox

Research questions with the highest social stakes rarely fit inside a single discipline.

Climate adaptation demands atmospheric science, but also behavioural psychology, political economy, and infrastructure engineering. Pandemic preparedness requires epidemiology alongside supply-chain logistics, risk communication, and the sociology of institutional trust. The governance of artificial intelligence spans computer science, law, philosophy, and organisational behaviour – boundaries which will continue to shift as the technology does.

Yet the machinery of academic production remains organised around siloed disciplines. Researchers are hired into departments, promoted through discipline-specific tenure criteria, and rewarded for publishing in journals whose reviewers share their assumptions. Funding panels, too, often privilege proposals that speak fluently to an established literature over those that attempt translation across fields. The result is a persistent paradox: we recognise that complex problems require interdisciplinary synthesis, but we design studies that fit neatly into disciplinary boxes.

This is not a failure of individual intent. Most researchers understand that neighbouring fields hold relevant insights. There are several components to the problem.

First, researchers face what Herbert Simon (1990) called bounded rationality: finite time and attention in the face of an ever-expanding universe of diverse information. Even a motivated scholar cannot monitor every adjacent field. The cost of exploration is simply too high.

Even when researchers do venture into adjacent fields, a deeper problem awaits. Disciplines aren’t just collections of findings organized under different keywords, they constitute distinct epistemic cultures (Knorr-Cetina, 1999). They have different standards of evidence, different relationships between theory and data, and different practices of warranting claims. A researcher who successfully locates relevant work in an unfamiliar field may still struggle to evaluate it: what counts as strong evidence or rigorous methodology by the standards of that discipline? What assumptions are so foundational that they go unstated? These questions are difficult to answer without extended immersion in a field's practices – precisely the investment that time constraints make impractical.

Consider how a traditional literature search unfolds. A researcher investigating vaccine hesitancy, for example, begins with familiar keywords in a database (PubMed, Google Scholar, Scopus, etc.). The search returns thousands of results. To manage this volume, the researcher filters instinctively: familiar journals, familiar authors, familiar terminology. A paper grounded in game theory or group identity might surface, but without the background knowledge to assess its relevance quickly, it is likely skipped in favour of something closer to home.

This filtering is not laziness. It is a necessary heuristic under constraint. But the effect is that researchers default to what they already know – not because other literatures are irrelevant, but because evaluating them is expensive. Curiosity has a labour cost, and that cost is paid disproportionately by those who try to work across boundaries.

Path Dependency in Research Questions

The consequences of cognitive constraint are most acute at the start of a project, because research questions exhibit path dependency. The initial framing of a question acts as a steering mechanism: once a research question is articulated in the language of one discipline, it shapes which variables appear relevant, which methods seem appropriate, which data get collected, and which audiences the work is written for.

Let’s follow our vaccine hesitancy example. This can be framed as a problem of information deficit (public health), as a problem of trust and institutional legitimacy (sociology), as a problem of incentive design (behavioural economics), or as a problem of identity and group membership (social psychology). Each of these disciplinary framings foreground different mechanisms, different evidence standards, and different implications.

Once a project begins with a narrowed frame, it rarely recovers its breadth. Retrofitting interdisciplinarity after data collection is difficult. If a study is designed around public health metrics alone, it cannot easily incorporate sociological variables about institutional trust if those variables were never conceptualised at the outset. The "narrow start" becomes a locked-in trajectory.

This is where existing computational tools fall short. Most AI-assisted literature review systems focus on later-stage tasks: screening papers against inclusion criteria, extracting data from studies already identified, or summarising bodies of work the researcher has already found. These tools improve efficiency within a search strategy, but they do not challenge the strategy itself. The gap is upstream, at the moment when the boundaries of inquiry are drawn.

Shifting the Economics of Inquiry

GenAI offers a mechanism to change this picture. Large language models (LLMs) can efficiently scan extensive literatures, surface unfamiliar terminology, and juxtapose concepts that are rarely connected.

Recent empirical work supports this possibility. Kusumegi et al. (2025), analysing scientific production across major repositories, found that researchers who adopted LLMs did not narrow their focus to an algorithmic mean. Instead, LLM adoption was associated with a more diversified discovery process: users cited more books, younger scholarship, and a broader range of literature than their non-AI-using counterparts. The authors conclude that LLMs helped researchers "overcome obstacles in discovering pertinent literature" that might otherwise have been missed due to time constraints or keyword mismatches.

But the same research highlights a risk. Kusumegi et al. (2025) also found that LLM adoption is associated with an erosion of a traditional heuristic: writing complexity becomes a less reliable signal of scientific quality. When surface-level polish is cheap to produce, it no longer proxies for conceptual rigour. Complex, fluent prose can disguise weak contributions.

More fundamentally, LLMs left unguided reproduce the biases present in their training data and, notoriously, fabricate references. A tool that can surface unfamiliar literatures can also invent them. This is not a minor technical glitch; it is an epistemic hazard. If researchers treat LLM outputs as authoritative rather than provisional, they risk building studies on foundations that do not exist.

The Intervention Point

The highest-return moment for interdisciplinary intervention is at the start of a project, before hypotheses harden and research questions lock in. GenAI makes it newly feasible to sample adjacent literatures at this stage, but without a structured method it can just as easily reproduce siloed thinking or introduce fabricated claims.

What is needed is a principled workflow that redirects AI's speed into a form that expands the researcher's conceptual field of vision while preserving rigour and transparency. We propose the Cross-Field Inquiry Engine in the following section as one such method.

2The Tool Landscape and the Missing Layer

Horseless Carriages and the Shape of New Tools

When a new technology emerges, the first generation of tools built upon it almost always mimics the old way of working. Koomen (2025) describes these early iterations as "horseless carriages": just as the first automobiles retained the high wheels, wooden benches, and upright posture that made sense for horse-drawn vehicles, early AI research tools often consist of legacy workflows with a chat window attached to the side.

The design mistake becomes obvious only in retrospect. The automotive engine demanded a redesign of the vehicle itself: a lower centre of gravity, enclosed cabin, and steering wheel rather than reins. In the current landscape of AI-enhanced research tools, the equivalent pattern is a chat box layered onto search, or a "write me a literature review" button layered onto reading. These integrations can feel impressive in a demonstration, yet they preserve the old constraints: the researcher must already know the right keywords, the right disciplinary categories, or the right framing in order to benefit.

This section maps the current tool ecosystem not to rank products, but to identify a structural gap. The question is not "Which tool is best?" The question is "Which stages of the research process are being strengthened, and which remain under-supported?"

Retrieval Support Versus Inquiry Support

A useful diagnostic distinction runs through the landscape. Retrieval support helps researchers find, screen, and process literature once a topic, query, or set of inclusion criteria has been specified. Inquiry support helps researchers widen and test the framing before the research question becomes path dependent.

Most AI-enhanced research tools cluster around retrieval. In a systematic review of 21 leading platforms, Bolaños et al. (2024) found that the predominant application of AI concerns the screening phase: machine learning classifiers trained iteratively on user-selected papers, designed to filter thousands of candidate citations into a manageable corpus. Tools such as Rayyan, Covidence, and ASReview exemplify this pattern. They are engines of convergence, and for systematic evidence synthesis—where the research question is fixed and the task is to identify all relevant studies—convergence is exactly the point.

The gap CF-IE targets sits earlier, and it is structurally different. Design-stage work requires divergence: the deliberate widening of theoretical lenses, constructs, and disciplinary starting points before search terms and inclusion criteria solidify. When a project starts narrow, tools optimised for convergence can inadvertently reinforce the initial frame. They are excellent at processing what the researcher already knows how to ask for, but less helpful at surfacing the "unknown unknowns" that would change the question itself.

This limitation becomes concrete when we consider how keyword retrieval actually works. If a researcher investigating vaccine hesitancy does not know that sociologists study "institutional trust" or that behavioural economists study "present bias," keyword algorithms will not surface those literatures. The terminology barrier is real. Amer and Elboghdadly (2024) describe this as a structural constraint of the "Search Engine Era": query-result-refine loops assume the user can formulate the right query in the first place.

GenAI offers a partial escape. LLMs can identify conceptual similarities even when the vocabulary differs, because they operate on semantic relationships rather than exact string matches. But the escape is only partial: a chat interface bolted onto a search engine still requires the user to know what to ask. The horseless carriage keeps its anachronistic wooden wheels.

The table reveals a consistent pattern. Current tools have genuine strengths, particularly for accelerating established workflows and lowering barriers to entry through natural language interfaces. But those same strengths can become weaknesses for research design if they encourage premature convergence, obscure the provenance of ideas, or assume that the user arrives with a well-formed question.

The row closest to CF-IE's ambitions is the last: concept mapping and sensemaking aids. These kinds of tools do support exploratory navigation, and they can surface unexpected connections. But they remain bounded by citation networks, which reflect existing disciplinary silos. A paper in organisational behaviour that addresses the same phenomenon as a paper in computer science may share no citations at all. These tools also lack structured protocols for forcing the researcher to engage with what they find.

What CF-IE Adds

Systematic review platforms, semantic search engines, or writing assistants can accelerate retrieval, screening, and drafting within disciplinary frames. What it does not reliably do is force early engagement with lenses that would change the design itself. CF-IE is not a competitor to the current tool ecosystem. It is designed to sit upstream and reshape the inputs those tools depend on.

The framework claims three additions to the current stack:

First, CF-IE supports semantic bridging across disciplinary vocabularies. When adjacent fields study similar phenomena using different terms, keyword retrieval and even conversational search can fail unless the user already knows the alternative constructs. CF-IE is designed to surface those alternatives early, before search terms and inclusion criteria harden into path-dependent commitments.

Second, CF-IE treats synthesis as a design aid rather than a substitute for reading. The output is not a polished literature review. It is an organized set of possibilities: framings, mechanisms, and conflicts. The AI surfaces options, while the researcher judges their worth and decides what deserves deep reading.

Third, CF-IE intervenes against path dependency at the point where it is most reversible. Screening tools accelerate the workflow after the project has already committed to a framing. CF-IE focuses on the moment when a broader scan can still change the research question, the mechanism of interest, or the method choice.

3CF-IE Blueprint and Theory of Change

Designing for a Different Task

CF-IE is not a specific piece of software. It is a conceptual blueprint for harnessing generative AI to expand how research questions are framed at the very start of a project. The long-term vision is a dedicated interface where a researcher can enter an idea and see adjacent concepts surface across disciplines, with the researcher always deciding which connections warrant pursuit and which should be set aside. A bare bones version of CF-IE can be operationalised immediately through a "prompt ladder" (a structured sequence of prompts) that guides a general-purpose LLM through the operations the method requires.

What CF-IE Is

CF-IE is a conceptual blueprint for using AI to widen the framing of a research question before hypotheses harden. It is built for a specific moment in the research process: when a researcher has an idea, a tentative question, or a puzzling phenomenon, but has not yet committed to a single disciplinary vocabulary, dataset, or method.

The method treats that moment as disproportionately influential. Early framing decisions guide later choices about variables, evidence, and result descriptions. A project that begins narrow tends to stay narrow. CF-IE intervenes at the point where reframing is relatively low-cost.

If part of the reason interdisciplinarity fails is the prohibitively high labour required to map adjacent fields, it is worth trying to redirect GenAI's processing power away from "answering" and toward "mapping," The goal is to have it generate a structured menu of possibilities – disciplines, constructs, mechanisms, conflicts – that a human can then evaluate, discard, or pursue through reading.

The Investigator in Charge

The defining characteristic of the CF-IE is the locus of control. Rather than viewing AI integration as a binary choice between manual work and automation, we adopt the Human Agency Scale (HAS) proposed by Shao et al. (2025), which visualizes a spectrum of collaboration ranging from AI-driven execution (H1) to human-driven completion (H5).

The traditional AI assistant model pushes users toward H1 or H2 dynamics, effectively acting as a supervisor who merely approves the machine's work. This dynamic risks generic outputs, decreased critical engagement, and a gradual erosion of the researcher's epistemic ownership over their own project.

In contrast, the CF-IE blueprint anchors the research process firmly at the human-driven end of the scale. The machine broadens the field of vision; the human chooses where to focus. The machine surfaces candidate constructs, mechanisms, and conflicts; the human decides which are worth pursuing, which require verification, and which should be discarded. The machine never authors a truth claim. It only proposes hypotheses for human evaluation. In this configuration, the AI possesses high capability but operates under strict human direction.

From Blueprint to Prototype

The long-term vision for CF-IE is a dedicated software interface, a platform where cross-field exploration is made visible, navigable, and auditable. But CF-IE does not depend on that interface existing. A minimal viable product can be implemented today using any general-purpose LLM and a structured sequence of prompts. This lean prototype, the prompt ladder, is the immediately actionable version of CF-IE.

The prompt ladder breaks the complex cognitive task of "research design" into discrete, verifiable rungs. Each rung has a specific purpose (divergence, translation, synthesis, grounding), can produce a specific output, and requires a specific human judgement before the researcher proceeds to the next step. By decomposing the task, the ladder prevents the model from drifting into sycophancy or hallucination. It forces the model to show its work at each stage, and it forces the researcher to evaluate that work before moving on.

The outputs of a CF-IE run might include:

  • A cross-field concept map listing adjacent disciplines, theories, and constructs that plausibly bear on the phenomenon

  • A translation memo recording definitional conflicts and measurement assumptions attached to key terms across fields

  • Candidate reframings of the research question, with explicit trade-offs noted for each option

  • A verification to-do list specifying targeted reading and checking tasks that turn model suggestions into actionable next steps

  • A disclosure-ready workflow record documenting how GenAI was used and what human decisions were made, supporting transparency and auditability

Scope Conditions

CF-IE is strongest when three conditions hold. First, the phenomenon under study is cross-field by nature, spanning disciplinary boundaries in ways that a single-field framing would miss. Second, the project is early enough that reframing can still change the design; if data collection is already underway, the window for CF-IE has largely closed. Third, the researcher can commit to diligent reading of literatures and verification of model outputs.

CF-IE is less helpful when the research question is already fixed by a formal protocol (e.g. a pre-registered replication study), when the primary goal is exhaustive recall of a definable corpus (e.g. a Cochrane-style systematic review), or when the researcher cannot verify outputs due to factors like time and access.

This scope statement matters for the ethical argument developed later in the chapter. CF-IE lowers the cost of exploration, but it does not remove the responsibility to justify claims. A researcher who uses CF-IE to surface a promising construct from an unfamiliar field still needs to read the primary literature before building that construct into their design. The method accelerates the discovery of what sources to consider reading; it does not substitute for reading.

4Prompt Ladder Toolkit for Cross-Field Design

CF-IE’s immediately usable form is a structured sequence of prompts — a prompt ladder — that surfaces nearby literatures, flags definitional and methodological clashes, and supports developing a sharper, better-grounded research question. The ladder is staged rather than a single prompt because staging reduces premature convergence, makes model outputs easier to verify rung by rung, and creates a visible record of human judgement at each step. Each rung produces a specific artefact and requires a specific decision before proceeding.

Each rung below follows the same structure: the purpose of the step, brief instructions for the researcher, the prompt to paste into a general-purpose LLM (with project-specific placeholders in brackets), and what a strong output looks like. A carry-forward notation closes each rung to make the cumulative chain explicit. A Session Setup document sits outside the ladder and is pasted once at the start of a session to establish model behaviour throughout.

Session Setup

Complete the bracketed fields with your specific project details and paste this document once before running Rung 1. If institutional or publication policies governing GenAI use apply to your project, include them in the normative constraints field. On a second pass of the ladder, update the project focus to reflect what reading has changed.

Session Setup

You are assisting with early-stage research design. Do not invent citations. Do not claim a finding is supported unless it is verifiable in identifiable sources. Treat all outputs as provisional until verified. If I share policies that prohibit generative AI use, raise this and do not continue.

Project focus: [Topic, setting, population].

Home discipline: [Discipline].

Goal: Expand the field of view before committing to a research question and design.

Non-negotiables:

  • Surface at least three dissenting framings from outside my home discipline.
  • Flag definitional conflicts and differences in evidence standards for key terms.
  • Label speculative or synthesised ideas [Generative] and claims traceable to identifiable sources [Verifiable].
  • Produce design artefacts — tables, maps, checklists — not polished prose.
  • Keep a record-ready structure: assumptions, mechanisms, measures, threats to validity, ethical risks, verification tasks.

Normative constraints and foreseeable harms: [Insert ethical boundaries, institutional policies, or AI use guidelines. When uncertain, name what would need to be checked rather than filling in the gap.]

Rung 1: Divergent Scan

Purpose: Break the narrow start by surfacing which disciplines plausibly study the phenomenon before any frameworks, search terms, or theoretical vocabularies are fixed.

For the researcher: When you receive the table, choose two or three disciplines whose critiques are hardest to dismiss — not the most familiar, but those that challenge the assumptions behind your initial framing. Verify any unfamiliar discipline in a standard scholarly database before carrying it forward.

Rung 1

Given my project focus, list six disciplines outside [home discipline] that study this phenomenon. For each discipline, provide:

  • The core theoretical lens.
  • Typical methods and evidence standards.
  • One critique this discipline would raise about my initial framing.

Return the output as a table.

What to look for: The critique column is the highest-value output. A strong critique challenges assumptions behind the framing, not just the method. If none requires you to rethink your starting point, ask: “Which discipline would most fundamentally contest the framing I started with, and why?”

Carry forward to Rung 2: The two or three disciplines whose critiques most destabilise your initial framing, and the specific critique each raises. This language constrains mechanism generation in Rung 3.

Rung 2: Translating Constructs

Purpose: Surface definitional conflicts and mismatched measurement traditions before they become invisible design errors.

For the researcher: Run this rung in two steps. The first prompt asks the model to identify terms that appear across your selected disciplines — including the same phenomenon named differently in different fields. Review those candidates, select two or three that span multiple vocabularies, and add any home-discipline constructs you plan to use as key terms. Then run the translation prompt. If you supply only home-discipline-specific frameworks in step two, cells for the adjacent disciplines will be speculative approximations rather than genuine translations. [Generative] labels in the output signal this; treat those cells with extra caution.

Rung 2, Step 1 — Candidate terms

Based on the Rung 1 disciplines and my project focus, list terms or constructs that appear in more than one of these disciplines’ vocabularies, including terms that study the same phenomenon under different names. Present as a table: the term, which disciplines use it, and what they call it in each field.

Rung 2, Step 2 — Translation table

For the following terms, compare how each of my selected disciplines defines them: [insert selected terms].

For each term, provide:

  • Definition in each discipline.
  • Typical operationalisations or measures.
  • What would count as strong evidence.
  • Common failure modes when translating across fields.

Return as a table. Label speculative cells [Generative].

What to look for: The failure modes row is the most important output. A strong entry names the specific design error that results from importing a construct across fields without acknowledging the definitional difference. If the failure modes are vague, ask: “What specific design error would a researcher make if they used [term] as defined in [discipline A] but interpreted results through [discipline B]?”

Carry forward to Rung 3: The conflict register: which terms carry non-interchangeable definitions, which operationalisations are incompatible, and how you plan to handle each conflict. These conflicts directly constrain Rung 3 mechanism generation.

Rung 3: Mechanisms and Causal Stories

Purpose: Make competing explanations explicit so that design choices are attached to mechanisms rather than vocabulary alone.

For the researcher: Before pasting the prompt, write one sentence describing the mechanism your home discipline would propose for this phenomenon. After receiving the output, check whether that expectation was confirmed or challenged. If the home discipline mechanism simply restates your starting assumption, ask: “What mechanism from my home discipline do the adjacent disciplines most directly falsify?”

Rung 3

Using the construct conflicts from Rung 2 as constraints, propose one plausible mechanism explaining the phenomenon for each discipline carried forward. For each mechanism, provide:

  • A causal chain in plain language.
  • What observable traces the mechanism would produce.
  • What kind of data could rule it out.

Then identify any significant alternative mechanisms from disciplines not carried forward. Flag these as overlooked alternatives.

Return mechanisms as a structured map and falsification conditions as a checklist.

What to look for: The falsification conditions are the most important output. A strong mechanism specifies what evidence would rule it out, not just what would support it. If two mechanisms produce identical traces and identical falsification conditions, they are the same idea in different disciplinary language, not competing explanations.

Carry forward to Rung 4: The full mechanism map and falsification checklist. Overlooked alternatives should be retained as live counterfactuals, not discarded.

Rung 4: Research Design Pathways

Purpose: Translate conceptual breadth into concrete design options, each grounded in a distinct mechanism and each falsifiable in different ways.

For the researcher: Run this rung in two steps. The context prompt gives the model your actual research constraints; without it, feasibility assessments will be generic and unreliable. After receiving the three options, do not select one immediately. Share them with a colleague from outside your home discipline before committing. This is the recommended pause point — the moment where external input can still change the question, the mechanism, or the method. Once you select a primary option, document in writing why you chose it and why you set aside the others. The unchosen options are counterfactuals, available as pivots if the primary design proves unfeasible.

Rung 4, Step 1 — Research constraints

Before proposing design options, I want to share my research constraints:

  • Institutional context: [university, department, infrastructure access].
  • Collaborators or fieldwork access: [describe].
  • Timeline: [approximate].
  • Data access: [what you can realistically obtain].
  • Budget: [rough range, or note if unfunded].

Use these when assessing feasibility in the design options you propose.

Rung 4, Step 2 — Design options

Propose three research design options, each grounded in a different mechanism from the Rung 3 mechanism map. For each option, specify:

  • Research question.
  • Unit of analysis.
  • Data sources.
  • Method approach (qualitative, quantitative, or mixed).
  • Threats to validity and mitigations.
  • What evidence would change your mind about this framing.

Close with a comparison table covering feasibility, ethical complexity, and what each option cannot explain. After presenting the options, do not recommend one — ask what constraints or priorities should guide the selection.

What to look for: Each option should test a distinct mechanism with different falsification implications. If two options look structurally similar — same method, same data, different keywords — they are not testing different mechanisms. If the feasibility assessments are identical across options despite the constraints you provided, ask the model to revise with those constraints explicitly applied.

Recommended pause: Before selecting a design option, share all three with a colleague from outside your home discipline. This is the moment in the ladder where external input can still change the design’s trajectory. External critique received at Rung 6, after the brief is drafted, leaves little room for genuine reframing.
Carry forward to Rung 5: Your chosen design option with documented rationale. The unchosen options labelled as counterfactuals.

Rung 5: Verification Plan

Purpose: Convert model suggestions into a targeted reading plan, separating idea generation from claims about the literature.

For the researcher: This is the hand-off point. After pasting the prompt, treat every output as a to-do list, not a completed review. Every anchor literature candidate is a search target until you have located and read it yourself. Complete the falsification tasks before moving to Rung 6. If the falsification search returns substantial evidence against your chosen mechanism, return to the Rung 4 counterfactuals and activate a secondary option before proceeding.

Rung 5

For [chosen design option], generate a verification plan that includes:

  • Search strings for each relevant discipline, drawing on the Rung 2 construct translation table to capture discipline-specific vocabulary for the same concepts. Use Boolean operators suitable for scholarly databases.
  • Inclusion criteria for a first-pass reading set.
  • A list of anchor literatures to locate, labelled [Candidate — unverified].
  • A falsification checklist: at least three searches specifically targeting evidence that would disconfirm the mechanism this design tests. Label these explicitly as falsification tasks.

What to look for: The falsification tasks are the most important output. If the checklist contains only searches designed to support the chosen design, ask: “What searches would I run specifically trying to find evidence that this mechanism is wrong?” Also check that the search strings use terminology from the Rung 2 translation table, not only home-discipline vocabulary.

Carry forward to Rung 6: Completed verification tasks and any design adjustments that resulted from your reading. If falsification tasks required returning to Rung 4, update your record before proceeding.

Rung 6: Design Brief

Purpose: Produce a shareable design artefact that demonstrates cross-field diligence without pretending the literature has been fully reviewed or the design finalised.

For the researcher: This rung produces two documents from two separate prompts. The full record is your internal audit trail and the basis for any AI use disclosure in a publication or grant submission. The two-page brief is what you share with the outside-discipline colleague. Do not attempt to produce both in a single prompt — they serve different audiences and collapsing them produces a document that does neither job well.

Rung 6, Step 1 – Full record prompt

Using all outputs from this ladder run, draft a full design record that includes:

  • Framing options considered and why one was selected, with notes on what each rejected option cannot explain.
  • Key construct definitions and translation notes from the Rung 2 conflict register.
  • Mechanisms to test, with falsification conditions.
  • Proposed methods and data sources.
  • Threats to validity and mitigations.
  • Ethics and governance risks, including the normative constraints specified at setup.
  • A rung-by-rung summary of how generative AI was used and what human decisions were made at each step.

Label any claim not yet verified through reading as [Unverified].

Rung 6, Step 2 – Two-page brief prompt

From the full design record, produce a condensed two-page brief for a colleague from outside my home discipline. Include:

  • The research question selected and the two alternatives not chosen, in one paragraph.
  • The primary mechanism and its main competitor, in plain language.
  • Proposed methods and data sources, in two to three sentences.
  • The two most significant threats to validity and planned mitigations.
  • One paragraph on ethics and governance risks.
  • One sentence summarising how generative AI was used.

Do not include the full conflict register or the rung-by-rung record in this version.

What to look for: In the full record, every AI-generated claim not verified through reading should be labelled [Unverified]. If the record reads like a completed literature review rather than a scaffolded design with explicit uncertainty, the model has resolved ambiguities that should remain open — ask it to reinstate the uncertainty labels. In the two-page brief, check that the framing is legible to someone outside your field.

Running the Ladder in Practice

The ladder is designed to be usable in a single sitting and is most valuable when run twice. A first pass produces rapid reconnaissance: a lens map, conflict register, mechanism map, and a preliminary set of design options that identify what to read and who to consult. A second pass, after targeted reading, produces a more grounded design brief and a more defensible choice among options.

For researchers working under significant time constraints, a minimum viable run consists of the Session Setup, Rung 1, and Rung 3 only. This produces enough to identify whether the initial framing has significant blind spots and to name the most important alternative explanations. It is reconnaissance, not a finished design. Treating the minimum viable run as equivalent to the full ladder would undermine the epistemic rigour the full process is designed to support.

One recurring risk across all ladder runs: the sunk cost of the process. By Rung 5, a researcher who has generated a lens map, conflict register, mechanism map, and three design options has significant investment in the chosen framing. This makes falsification searching feel like a threat rather than a requirement. It is not. A falsification task that sends you back to Rung 4 is the system working correctly, not failing.

5Epistemic Risks and Ethical Safeguards

The prompt ladder addresses three predictable failure modes that arise when GenAI enters design-stage reasoning: hallucination, sycophancy, and bias reinforcement.

Hallucination in research workflows means fabricated citations and confident statements not entailed by the evidence. This risk is amplified when CF-IE asks the model to traverse unfamiliar fields where the user may lack the background to notice a plausible-sounding but invented reference. Structured prompting helps: decomposing tasks into discrete steps reduces hallucinated references compared to single-shot approaches, though it does not eliminate them (Agarwal et al., 2024). CF-IE’s staged verification requirements, particularly the verification plan and audit trail, aim to operationalize the distinction between a lead to investigate and a claim to cite.

Sycophancy is the tendency for a model to align with the user's premises even when those premises should be questioned. Gao et al. (2025), benchmarking LLM performance on peer review, found that models are susceptible to prompt manipulation (most exhibited score increases of one to two points when instructed to be positive) and that longer inputs were rated more favorably regardless of quality. This means that polished framing can be mistaken for stronger framing, and the model's evaluative stance is prompt-sensitive. The prompt ladder version addresses this through adversarial steps – Rung 1's critique requirement, Rung 3's falsification checklist – that prevent self-confirming inquiry from becoming the default.

Bias reinforcement occurs because models trained primarily on Western, English-language scholarship default to those perspectives. The heuristic anchoring discussion acknowledged this trade-off; the safeguard is a deliberate perspective gap check that names which populations, regions, or methodological traditions are likely missing from the scan.

Taken together, these safeguards – staged verification, adversarial prompting, and deliberate perspective audits – work to prevent fluent synthesis from being mistaken for justified inference.

The ETHICAL Framework as a Lens

The ETHICAL framework proposed by Eacersall et al. (2024) offers a useful lens for understanding how CF-IE's design choices support responsible practice. The framework's seven principles map onto specific features of the workflow:

E - Examine policies and guidelines. Before starting work, the engine guides users to provide the model with any policies or guidelines (from their institute/department, publication, etc.) that they are required to follow and considers that throughout its work (e.g. if GenAI is prohibited, it should refuse to continue).

T - Think about social impacts. The perspective gap check directly addresses this: recording which voices are likely missing due to language, indexing, or institutional bias.

H - Harness understanding of the technology. The staged structure forces engagement with model limitations (recency constraints, retrieval gaps, distinction between generation and evidence, etc.) rather than treating outputs as authoritative.

I - Indicate use. CF-IE's design trail makes institutional disclosure requirements straightforward to meet, as the researcher can document exactly how GenAI shaped the design process.

C - Critically engage with outputs. Every step requires human judgment before proceeding. The verification plan step converts model suggestions into targeted reading rather than accepted claims.

A - Access secure versions. The prompt ladder version of CF-IE encourages users to confirm model security before use and the future version will incorporate additional data security measures.

L - Look at user agreements. The prompt ladder version of CF-IE encourages users to confirm user agreement understanding and the future version will incorporate additional input usage constraints and expectation setting.

CF-IE was designed with responsible AI implementation as one of its three core barriers. The ETHICAL framework helps articulate why the prompt ladder's structure matters: when safeguards are embedded in the workflow rather than appended as disclaimers, responsible practice becomes achievable under real time constraints.

6Conclusion

Evaluation

If CF-IE is to be more than an attractive story, it needs evaluation that matches its purpose. The goal is not to prove that CF-IE creates better science in general. That would confuse proximal outcomes (better early-stage framing) with distal outcomes (publication, citation, policy impact) shaped by external factors.

Three near-term questions test whether CF-IE improves design-stage cognition under realistic constraints:

Does CF-IE widen the field of view in a traceable way? The simplest measure is whether a design brief includes disciplinary lenses, constructs, or mechanisms absent from a baseline draft, plus a verification plan rather than unsupported claims.

Does CF-IE reduce avoidable translation failures? A cross-field panel—one reviewer from the home discipline, one from an adjacent field, one methodologist—can assess whether the conflict register identifies definitional clashes early enough to prevent category errors later.

Does CF-IE preserve epistemic responsibility under speed? Because GenAI makes fluent text easy to generate, the risk is that teams move faster while becoming less certain what is grounded. Evaluation should treat verification gates as a behavioral outcome: do researchers actually perform verification tasks, label uncertainty, reject weak suggestions, document what they did?

We plan to publish more about these evaluations as both CF-IE as a technology and its adoption continues to develop.

From Practice to Norm

A long term goal that CF-IE seeks to contribute to is shifting cross-field diligence from a personal technique to a shared expectation. CF-IE does not remove incentives like departmental boundaries, tenure logics, or reviewer conservatism. But it does create a pathway for cultural change by making a desirable practice cheap enough to repeat, structured enough to teach, and legible enough to evaluate.

There is also a structural reason this matters now. Kusumegi et al. (2025) found that LLM adoption is associated with an erosion of a traditional heuristic: writing complexity as a reliable signal of academic quality. When surface-level polish is cheap to produce, it no longer proxies for conceptual rigour. This has major implications for how institutions assess quality, and we believe will soon shift attention upstream. If reviewers can no longer lean on prose as a proxy for rigor, they will need other legible signals: a transparent account of how a research question was framed, what alternatives were considered, what assumptions were stress-tested.

One concrete proposal: a cross-field scan appendix as a norm for interdisciplinary publishing. Just as systematic reviews require PRISMA statements to document search strategy, interdisciplinary papers could include a brief appendix documenting which disciplines were scanned, which definitions were contested, and why the specific framing was chosen.

A Bounded Claim

The claim of this chapter is that CF-IE makes a specific form of early-stage interdisciplinarity more practical: a structured cross-field scan that occurs before the project locks in, yields inspectable artefacts, forces translation, requires verification, and keeps the investigator in charge.

The aspiration is cultural. Interdisciplinarity has often functioned as a luxury afforded to researchers with time, institutional security, or large teams. CF-IE does not change the incentive system, but it changes the marginal cost of behaving differently. When the initial scan of neighboring fields becomes a thirty-minute to half-day task rather than a weeks-long exploration with uncertain payoff, more researchers will actually attempt it. The scan is not the whole of interdisciplinary work (evaluation and integration remain labor-intensive) but it removes a barrier that currently stops many projects before they start. When the result is legible to others, it can be recognized. When it can be recognized, it can be taught, then expected.
